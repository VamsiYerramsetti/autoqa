Auto Q&A

How answers work

Last updated 12 November 2026

  • Answers are written by an automated system, not by a person and not by the speaker.
  • They are based on what the speaker gave us: slides, notes, and their replies.
  • If the material doesn't say it, you are told so and can send the question to the speaker.
  • Answers can be wrong. The speaker has the last word.

What writes the answers

A language model called Claude, made by the company Anthropic. A language model is a computer program that reads text and writes text. It is fast and reads carefully, but it is not a person, does not know the speaker, and can make mistakes.

What it is given

Each time someone asks a question, the model receives:

  • the question, and the few questions that person asked just before it;
  • the text of the speaker's slides and any documents or notes they added;
  • what the speaker said when setting up the talk (a short conversation about what the slides leave out);
  • replies the speaker has already given to earlier questions at this talk;
  • rules: answer briefly and plainly, only state facts about the talk that the material supports, say which slide the answer rests on, and never guess.

It is not given your name, your email, or anything about you.

What it does with a question

  • The material answers it: you get a short answer and see the slide it comes from.
  • It's a general question (what a term means, how a method usually works): it may answer from general knowledge, and says clearly that this part is not from the talk.
  • It's about this talk, and the material doesn't say: it tells you so instead of guessing, and you can send the question to the speaker, with your name or anonymously.
  • The speaker replies: the reply appears in your chat, and the next person who asks the same thing gets the speaker's own words.

It is not "trained" on the talk

The model does not learn from the slides or from your questions. It is handed the material again each time and keeps nothing afterwards. Anthropic's commercial terms do not allow it to train its models on this content. One talk's material is never used to answer questions at another talk.

What can go wrong

  • It can misread a slide, especially charts and pictures: today it reads the text of slides, not the images.
  • It can state general background that is out of date or not quite right.
  • It can miss that something is covered, or say more than the slide supports.

We test it every day with made-up talks and audiences, score every answer, and fix what goes wrong. Even so: for anything that matters, check the slide shown with the answer, or ask the speaker.

Who can see your question

The speaker can see the questions asked at their talk, without names. They see your name and email only if you choose to send a question with them. Others in the room can see questions waiting for the speaker, never who asked. Details are in the privacy notice.
